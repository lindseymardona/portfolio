Before and after

The problem

Rigorous data doesn’t move policy on its own.

LPPI had rigorous data on how extreme heat and air pollution fall unevenly on California’s Latino neighborhoods, down to the census tract. Researchers can parse dense tables easily. The advocates, organizers, and legislative staff who actually drive change usually can’t, and they don’t have time to try.

The design challenge was legibility under pressure: make a neighborhood’s disparities clear to a non-researcher in under a minute, on a printed two-page factsheet, without diluting the research.

Who it’s for

People who use data to argue for change, not to study it.

  • Advocates and community organizers who bring statistics to city council meetings, community workshops, and grant proposals.
  • Legislative and agency staff who need a defensible number fast, often in a briefing.
  • Researchers and partner organizations who want neighborhood-level evidence in one place.

Many of them would meet the factsheets on paper, printed and handed across a table. That meant designing for print: two pages, no hover states, text no smaller than 12 pt, and a real question of how everything would read when printed in black and white.

Team and my role

The only designer on a team of researchers.

My eight-person team: six researchers and project leads, a developer, and me as the only designer. I owned the interaction and visual design of the interactive county map, the website, and every factsheet.

  • Design: factsheets for both topics, the website and homepage, the “Our Data,” FAQ, and Resource Directory pages, and the color and type system.
  • Research: user testing was part of my scope from the start. I co-planned the comprehension survey with our project lead and integrated stakeholder and user feedback into each iteration.
  • Handoff: I worked closely with our developer as he automated factsheet generation from my Figma files, so every layout decision had to work across all 46 county factsheets, not just one.

I’m acknowledged by name in the dashboard’s official Technical Documentation for the design of the factsheets.

The experience

Pick a county, get the disparities in seconds.

An interactive map of California counties. Selecting a county opens two infographic-style factsheets, one for extreme heat and one for air pollution, each contrasting Latino neighborhoods with non-Latino White neighborhoods on every metric. Final scope: 23 counties plus statewide California factsheets, each produced for both topics.

Around the factsheets, I designed the supporting pages that make the data trustworthy: “Our Data,” where I proposed a searchable, filterable indicator list (the shipped version sorts alphabetically with theme filters), an FAQ, and a Resource Directory of community organizations. I also pushed for a short explanatory blurb on the homepage, pointing to dashboards like KFF and MyFutureNC, because a landing page with no text gives a first-time visitor no way to know what they’re looking at. A one-sentence version shipped: it tells visitors the dashboard equips advocates and decision-makers with data on climate and health risks in Latino neighborhoods.

Testing what people actually understood

We asked people to find things, not whether they liked it.

I co-planned a comprehension survey with our project lead. Following advice from LPPI’s Latino Data Hub team, we built it around tasks instead of opinions: people can tell you they understand something without being able to use it. Thirty-eight people tested a draft factsheet for LA or Fresno County: advocates and community organizers, state agency staff, UCLA researchers, and LPPI staff. Each one:

  1. Found the definition of an extreme heat day.
  2. Compared Latino and non-Latino White neighborhoods on a chart.
  3. Read the map to say whether the county had more or fewer extreme heat days than the state.
  4. Rated their confidence and the design, then told us what was confusing or missing.

The map was the problem.

Everyone found the definition and made the neighborhood comparison. The map was different: on the LA version, 4 of 34 people got the county-versus-state comparison wrong or couldn’t find it, and 5 more said it was hard to find.

People didn’t know when they’d misread it.

7 of those 9 still rated themselves confident they understood the factsheet. If we had only asked people how they felt, we would have missed a problem that affected about a quarter of LA readers. One tester put it plainly: “I eventually understood the map, but not at first glance like everything else.”

The comments explained why.

  • The legend said “above” and “below average” without saying which average: county or state.
  • “Latino neighborhood” was never defined on the page.
  • Page 2 was dense, and its stats were loosely connected to their labels.
  • People wanted plain-language takeaways, more visual contrast, and less text.

How the factsheet evolved

The hardest problem was restraint.

My first draft in December was text-heavy, and I flagged it myself: I wouldn’t go below 12 pt type, so the copy crowded out the numbers, which were the whole point. Across iterations I cut copy aggressively and rebuilt the hierarchy so the statistics carried the weight, using size, color, and directional indicators to steer the eye. That meant real editorial tradeoffs on both sides. Researchers rewrote and even removed carefully written copy, and I dropped graphs and visuals of my own, all to fit the essential data legibly in a fixed space.

Testing told us what to fix.

We prioritized fixes from the survey as a team, within real constraints: two pages of space, and an automated pipeline where every layout change had a cost. I:

  • defined both neighborhood types right on the page (census tracts with 70%+ Latino or non-Latino White residents),
  • moved the extreme heat definition into a banner at the top of the section,
  • pulled the county and state averages out of the map corner into their own callout,
  • added a subtitle tying the county statistics to heat exposure, and
  • rebuilt page 2 into labeled columns with plain-language cause-and-effect statements (“Less tree canopy = Increased exposure”).

The legend confusion surfaced again after launch, this time from our advisory committee, and the legend was relabeled to name the comparison explicitly: “Below County Avg.” and “Above County Avg.” Two sources, months apart, found the same problem.

Two early calls that testing backed up.

Before testing, I had asked whether the factsheets should define their comparison groups (the team chose to put definitions in the FAQ), and I had flagged my own draft as text-heavy. Testing confirmed both: undefined terms confused readers, and density was the most common complaint.

Iteration as system-building.

Extreme Heat10 rounds
Air Pollution4

The flagship Extreme Heat factsheet went through 10 rounds of revision because it was effectively inventing the template: every labeling, spacing, and hierarchy decision was being made for the first time. Air Pollution mostly reused that pattern and needed only 4.

Designing for automation.

Our developer generated every factsheet from my Figma designs, so a design change meant reworking his pipeline for all 46 county factsheets. We worked through it together: for example, instead of hand-placing each map legend, we set legend positions per county in the data file so automation wouldn’t break.

A late curveball on color.

The palette I designed used two distinct color families, one per topic, so extreme heat and air pollution read as different chapters at a glance. It went through 4 rounds of revisions and passed WCAG contrast testing against the university’s AA standard. Then, eight rounds into the factsheet’s revisions, leadership decided it read as too colorful and asked for a single, more subdued green-and-blue palette shared across both factsheets. It wasn’t the direction the team or I would have chosen, and I still think distinct palettes served the reader better, but the decision was final, so I made it work. Because I’d built reusable Figma color styles, swapping the palette across every factsheet was a near-instant global change.

A shifting brand.

Partway through, the dashboard moved from sharing its parent initiative’s branding to standing up its own visual identity. Re-skinning the system while factsheets were still in flight was exactly the situation reusable Figma styles and components are built for.

Outcome

Launched, used in policy rooms, and studied in a peer-reviewed journal.

The dashboard launched publicly on June 24, 2025, billed as the first tool of its kind to provide Latino neighborhood-level climate and health data across California. It surfaced stark, actionable disparities: for example, Latino neighborhoods are roughly twice as likely to face poor air quality and asthma-related ER visits compared with non-Latino White neighborhoods.

  • Launch: more than 500 people registered for the online community briefing, the Los Angeles Times covered it, and 745 people visited on launch day.
  • Research: the subject of a peer-reviewed Health Affairs article (2026; 45(5): 570–579).
  • Policy: the LA County factsheets went into the folders LPPI brought to five state legislative offices in Sacramento.
  • Convenings: presented at NALEO’s 43rd Annual Conference, and used in LPPI’s 2026 Policy in Action Summit and community “policy plática” convenings to ground advocacy discussions.
  • Reach: about 6,000 users in its first year.
  • Credit: acknowledged by name in the dashboard’s official Technical Documentation for the design of the factsheets.

During testing, a program manager for CalEnviroScreen, the state’s environmental justice screening tool, said OEHHA could link to the dashboard from CalEnviroScreen and share it with the state’s CalHeatScore team.

Follow-on work.

I went on to design statewide California factsheets, a state data brief, a policy toolkit, a mobile prototype, event stickers, and a vertical conference banner. I built the mobile version on a proper auto-layout and component system in Figma, applying what I’d just learned in Google’s UX Design Certificate, which made it noticeably more rigorous and maintainable than my original files for the web build.

What I learned

Measure what people do, not just what they say.

The confidence gap changed how I think about feedback. Ratings told us people felt fine; the tasks showed where they weren’t. I see the same gap in my teaching now: the exit tickets I designed for my sections pair confidence ratings with comprehension checks, and the two don’t always agree.

Re-test the fix.

We didn’t have time to re-run the tasks on the revised factsheets before launch, and the legend problem came back through our advisory committee. Next time I’d build a second round into the plan to confirm the changes worked instead of assuming they did.

Test the whole product, not just the artifact.

Budget and timeline put the factsheets first. I would have liked to run usability research on the website and map interaction too, since people use both.

Design systems from day one.

If I did it over, I’d set up a Figma component and color-token system at the start. I only did this well on the later mobile prototype, and the color-scheme rework is exactly the kind of pain a token system prevents.

Facilitation is design work.

Managing the tension between researchers’ desire to preserve their writing and the constraints of a legible page was as consequential as any single visual decision, and something I got measurably better at with each round.
