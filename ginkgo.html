Ginkgo

A therapist-matching app for Asian Americans that pairs a culturally aware directory with a “find your match” quiz, so finding the right care feels less overwhelming.

The problem

Finding a therapist who understands your culture is harder if you’re Asian American.

Stigma, language barriers, a shortage of culturally competent providers, and low awareness that better options exist all stack up. The numbers make it concrete: in 2022, only 9% of Asian American adults received mental health treatment, compared with 28% of white adults. Only about 4% of U.S. psychologists are Asian, a small pool of people likely to understand values like filial piety, emotional restraint, or collectivism without needing them explained. And about 72% of Asian Americans speak a language other than English at home, which matters in something as vulnerable as therapy.

The numbers don’t show where people fall out. The research did, in three ways:

Stigma often lives in the family.

Standard survey questions about stigma ask whether you worry what neighbors think. In many Asian communities, shame sits inside the family and is tied to saving face. So the reassurance people need is specific: who can see what, and whether anything shows up on a shared insurance statement. A generic “100% confidential” badge doesn’t answer that.

Many people arrive through the body first.

Asking a doctor about insomnia, headaches, or a racing heart carries none of the shame of naming anxiety or depression. An app that opens with “diagnose your mental illness” loses those people immediately.

The real failure point is session two.

In Sue’s foundational study of Seattle community mental health centers, about half of ethnic minority clients stopped after a single session, compared with 30% of white clients. Zane’s later research found the same pattern for Asian American clients specifically: more early dropout, shorter stays, and worse short-term outcomes.

That reframed the goal. Underutilization gets the headlines, but attrition is where care is actually lost. So I defined success as finding a therapist you’re still seeing at session three.

Who it’s for

Three people, so I wasn’t designing for one imaginary “Asian American user.”

I built three personas from the research, spread across age, immigration experience, and who the care is for:

  • Emily, 23, a burnt-out software engineer from a high-achieving immigrant family, wary of being seen as weak.
  • Kevin, 28, an auto mechanic looking for a Vietnamese-speaking therapist for his mother, who distrusts therapy.
  • Arjun, 15, a recent immigrant and therapy skeptic who needs a way in that doesn’t feel cold or clinical, and that his religious parents will allow.

Journey maps followed each of them from “maybe I need help” to “matched,” flagging every point where it got emotionally or practically hard.

Role

Solo, from research to a clickable prototype.

This was my capstone for the Grow with Google × Mentor Me Collective program, completed alongside a cohort working on their own capstones. I combined it with the program’s design-for-social-good project, which gave me about five weeks for the whole thing. I did every part of it myself:

  • Research: secondary research and data synthesis, plus a competitive audit of four platforms.
  • Synthesis: personas, user stories, empathy maps, and journey maps.
  • Design: sketches, low-fidelity wireframes, a component system, and a high-fidelity interactive prototype in Figma.
  • Ethics: a risk analysis of how a product like this could reinforce the problems it’s meant to solve.

Research

Grounded in data, then tested against what already exists.

I pulled data from national health databases, workforce registries, and census estimates. For smaller population segments, I used 5-year American Community Survey estimates, since they’re more reliable at that scale. Three priorities came out of it and drove every decision after:

  1. Language and access. Filter by language fluency and cultural background, with multilingual support built in.
  2. Trust and cultural relevance. Make therapy feel normal within Asian cultural frameworks, instead of translating a Western clinical tone.
  3. Simplicity. Plain language and visual cues, so ESL and older users don’t drop off.

The competitors were well designed. The gap was elsewhere.

I audited four platforms expecting to find usability problems. Three of the four rated outstanding on navigation, hierarchy, and structure.

All four interfaces are English-only.

Two findings drove everything after:

  • Cultural fit can’t be filtered anywhere. You can filter for a therapist who speaks Vietnamese, but not for one who understands why you can’t tell your mom you’re in therapy.
  • Language is treated as a therapist attribute, never a user need. Every platform lets you filter therapists by language, and none offers its own interface in another language. Even the directory built for Asian communities is English-only. For a population where 72% speak another language at home, that’s a major gap.

The design

Two ways in: browse, or let a short quiz narrow it down.

  • Explore therapists: a directory of therapist cards that show at a glance whether someone is accepting new clients and offers online or in-person sessions, plus real cultural filters. The centerpiece is a “Cultural Perspectives and Values” filter: 21 options in six groups (identity and worldview, family and social dynamics, performance and pressure, emotional expression, identity and inclusion, and cultural beliefs), covering experiences like saving face, language brokering, first-gen or immigrant experience, and not wanting to burden others. Practical filters sit alongside it: accepting new clients, price, insurance, language, faith, age, session type, and therapist gender.
  • Find your match: a short quiz that turns an overwhelming open search into a few gentle questions.

Profiles that mirror the filters.

Each profile opens with the therapist’s photo, credentials, location, session type, and a bio in their own words, with buttons to call, email, visit their website, share, or save. Below that, it’s organized into the same categories people filter by: values and client focus (cultural perspectives, the ages and groups they work with, languages with fluency levels, and faith), treatment approach, specialties, and finances, including fees, payment methods, and insurance. Because profiles and filters share one vocabulary, people can see exactly how a therapist lines up with what they selected.

A therapist profile: a bio in her own words, then the same values, languages, and logistics people filter by.

What I’d measure

the share of users still in care at session three, the point most people in this population never reach.

How the design evolved

From sketches to a branded prototype.

I mapped both flows and the filter menus as low-fidelity wireframes before adding any color, then built a component sheet so the screens stayed consistent as they multiplied. It was the first project where I used auto layout, reusable components, and responsive layouts properly, all things I’d just learned in the Google UX Design course.

A calm visual language, on purpose. I chose a ginkgo leaf, a soft sage palette, and copy like “your space to begin” so the app would feel welcoming instead of clinical. For someone already ambivalent about therapy, a screen that feels like a medical intake form is one more reason to close it.

Designing responsibly

A product about identity and stigma can make both worse.

  • “Asian American” isn’t one audience. A systematic review of 34 studies found that a lack of ethnically matched providers was a barrier specifically for Korean and Vietnamese participants, and that English proficiency predicted help-seeking across groups. The model minority myth, which treats Asian Americans as uniformly healthy, is part of why so little of this data exists. That’s why the personas span age, immigration experience, and background.
  • The race-as-proxy problem. People strongly prefer therapists of their own race or ethnicity: Cabral and Smith’s meta-analysis of 52 studies found a moderately strong preference (d = 0.63). However, the same analysis found that racial matching didn’t actually improve outcomes. Instead, what does predict outcomes is a therapist’s cultural humility. Indexing purely on race would also shrink an already small provider pool and reinforce the idea that only someone who looks like you can help. Ginkgo filters on cultural values, language, and faith, and every profile lists the therapist’s cultural perspectives, so people can choose based on attunement while still seeing who the therapist is.
  • Accessibility as safety. Multilingual options, low-stigma wording, and progressive disclosure: the filter panel tucks its many options into collapsible sections, so nothing appears all at once.

Outcome

A clickable prototype, presented as my capstone.

I delivered a full high-fidelity prototype that put the three research priorities into practice: cultural and language filtering, the find-your-match quiz, a language toggle covering several major Asian languages, and a simple, image-supported interface that uses Gestalt grouping to keep text short. I presented it to my Grow with Google × Mentor Me Collective cohort as the capstone that completed my Google UX Design Certificate.

Where I’d take it next.

  • Bring the community in. Co-design sessions with Asian Americans who have looked for therapy, then do usability testing with them. This is the first priority, and the gap I take most seriously: see below.
  • A youth “start here” flow for minors: features like anonymous resources, plain-language information on minor consent by state, and honest explainers of what therapy is, to build trust before anyone has to commit.
  • Account creation and a responsive web version.
  • A growing set of cultural filters and an updated resource library, and eventually partnerships with existing organizations. The values-based framework could also adapt to other communities.

What I’d change: rethinking the matching

Everything in this section came after the prototype was finished.

The prototype already had the filters and a choose-all-that-apply quiz. These four changes would connect them.

  • Concept

    Let people rank what matters.

    Add one quiz question: pick your two must-haves. Those get weighted heavily and the rest lightly, because what matters most changes with each person’s situation. Someone finding care for a parent may put language first; someone like Emily may put “won’t make me explain my family” first. Accommodating client preferences is linked to fewer dropouts (Swift and colleagues, 53 studies), which matters most in a population where attrition is the main failure point.

  • Concept

    Show why each match fits.

    Results would explain themselves, for example ✦ Works in Vietnamese ✦ Specializes in family relationships for Kevin’s mother. That’s Don Norman’s principle of feedback: answering the quiz should get a clear response, or people are back to guessing.

  • Concept

    Flag good fits while browsing.

    A “Suggested for you” badge on therapists who match your quiz answers, so the directory and the quiz work together. I’d be careful with a percentage score: in something this personal, “87% match” can feel more precise than the matching really is, so I’d test whether it builds trust or false confidence.

  • Concept

    Guide bios with prompts.

    Replace the open bio box with a few open-ended prompts, like what a first session with you looks like, what you’d want someone nervous about therapy to know, and what you’re still learning about working across cultures. Every profile would answer the same questions, still in each therapist’s own voice.

What I learned

Words matter.

Designing for something this stigmatized changed what good UX meant to me. The framing, the wording, and when to reveal what mattered as much as any layout decision.

Don’t flatten culture into fixed boxes.

Trying not to reduce people to categories pushed me toward flexible, values-based personalization. That’s a principle I’ll carry into anything that touches identity.

Sensitive topics need the community involved from the start.

Every decision here rests on published research, data, and a competitive audit. That grounded the design, but none of it came from the people it’s for, and that’s the biggest limit of this project. Literature tells you what’s true on average. It can’t tell you whether a quiz question feels safe to someone deciding whether to look for help, or whether a feature meant to help could end up causing harm. For something this personal and culturally loaded, testing at the end isn’t enough on its own. Next time I’d use participatory design: bringing Asian Americans who have looked for therapy into the process early to co-design the matching questions and profile prompts, then testing the result with them.

One question I’d bring to those sessions: should Ginkgo be for Asian Americans at all? A dedicated space builds trust, which is why the Asian Mental Health Collective’s directory had the strongest community framing in my audit. But a single tool for “Asian Americans” risks the same flattening I warn about above, and the matching model itself isn’t specific to any one group. A shared, multilingual, values-based platform with community-specific entry points might serve more people without losing that trust. Whether a dedicated space feels safer or more isolating is something only the people using it can answer.

Invisible decisions still shape the screens.

Leaving the matching logic unspecified made sense for a five-week prototype, since no one would see it on screen. But thinking it through later surfaced things the interface needed, like asking people to pick their must-haves and explaining why each match fits. Now I sketch the logic behind a feature early, even when it won’t be visible, because it changes what the visible parts should be.
